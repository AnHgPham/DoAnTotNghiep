\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

We study few-shot open-set keyword spotting (KWS): a single embedding encoder is
trained once on a large word vocabulary and is then deployed to recognize an
arbitrary user-defined keyword set from only a handful of enrollment examples,
while rejecting out-of-vocabulary speech and silence. We build a fully
reproducible pipeline around two compact encoders --- a depthwise-separable CNN
(DSCNN-L, $\approx$0.41\,M parameters) and a temporal-attention edge model
(EdgeSpot-Full $\tau{=}4$, $\approx$0.13\,M parameters) --- combined with three
feature front-ends (MFCC, log-mel, and a trainable per-channel PCEN) and four
metric-learning objectives (Triplet, Sub-center ArcFace (SCAF), Generalized
End-to-End (GE2E), and a cosine knowledge-distillation (KD) objective from a
frozen Wav2Vec\,2.0 teacher). All systems are trained on Multilingual Spoken
Words Corpus (MSWC) English and evaluated cross-corpus on Google Speech Commands
v2 (GSC) under a strict EdgeSpot-exact 10-shot open-set protocol with 100
randomized runs. Through a 16-configuration factorial screen we show that
(i) the trainable PCEN front-end is the single most reliable improvement (up to
$+9.2$ accuracy points at $1\%$ false-accept rate for the edge encoder);
(ii) the centroid-based GE2E objective dominates classification-style losses for
open-set generalization;
(iii) crucially, the classification objective SCAF collapses when the training
vocabulary grows to $37{,}387$ classes unless its loss weight is reduced by four
orders of magnitude.
We further characterize how accuracy saturates near two million training clips
and show that the cosine-KD objective reaches GE2E-level accuracy with roughly
half the data. The best overall configuration (DSCNN-L + PCEN + GE2E) attains
$86.36\%$ open-set accuracy at $1\%$ false-accept rate and $95.21$ AUC on GSC
test, while the best compact model (EdgeSpot-Full + PCEN + GE2E) reaches
$82.87\%$ at one third of the parameters. We release the complete code,
training/evaluation protocols, and an offline web demonstrator.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} keyword spotting, few-shot learning, open-set
recognition, metric learning, PCEN, GE2E, MSWC, Google Speech Commands.

\section{Context and Motivation}\label{sec:context}

Keyword spotting (KWS) --- the task of detecting a small set of spoken commands
in a continuous audio stream --- is the always-on entry point of nearly every
modern voice interface. Classical KWS systems are trained as closed-set
classifiers over a fixed vocabulary (e.g., the ten Google Speech Commands words).
This design has two structural limitations. First, the keyword set is frozen at
training time: supporting a new wake word or command requires collecting data and
retraining the model. Second, a softmax classifier is, by construction, not
open-set: every input is forced into one of the known classes, so unseen words
and background noise are misclassified as commands, producing false
activations. This work targets the more realistic and more difficult few-shot
open-set formulation. We train a single embedding encoder on a very large and
diverse word vocabulary, and at deployment time the end user defines a
keyword set by providing only a few ($k$) enrollment utterances per word. Each
keyword is represented by a prototype (the mean embedding of its enrollment
shots); recognition is performed by nearest-prototype matching in embedding
space, and an explicit open-set decision rejects anything that is too far from
all prototypes. Because the encoder is never retrained for a particular keyword,
the same model serves unlimited downstream vocabularies; because rejection is
distance-based, the system can abstain on out-of-vocabulary speech and silence.
The question we pursue throughout this thesis is what makes such an encoder
generalize and at what scale of the training vocabulary the
problem must be tackled --- whether a small vocabulary suffices or a large one is
required, and if so how large --- in order to achieve that generalization.

\paragraph{Contributions.} Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item A reproducible, dependency-light few-shot open-set KWS framework with two
compact encoders, three feature front-ends, four metric objectives, and four
open-set rejection heads (nearest-class-mean / OpenMax / energy / calibrated),
all sharing a common prototype interface (\Cref{ch:method}).
\item A 16-configuration factorial screen on full-vocabulary MSWC that isolates
the contribution of each axis with paired deltas; PCEN and GE2E emerge as the two
robust wins (\Cref{ch:results}).
\item A scale analysis showing:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em,itemsep=1pt]
\item Accuracy saturation near $\sim$2\,M training clips; and
\item The failure mode of Sub-center ArcFace at $37$k classes, with a
gradient-magnitude explanation and a practical fix (\Cref{ch:results}).
\end{itemize}
\item A knowledge-distillation study showing that a cosine objective against a
frozen Wav2Vec\,2.0 teacher matches GE2E accuracy with roughly half the training
data (\Cref{ch:results}).
\item A complete cross-corpus evaluation under a strict EdgeSpot-exact 10-shot
protocol (100 runs), together with a CPU streaming pipeline (Silero VAD +
smoothing state machine) and an offline web demonstrator (\Cref{ch:method}).
\end{itemize}

\section{The problem in plain terms}\label{sec:plain}
\reviewnote{New section --- a non-technical explanation of the problem written in
an objective register, about one page long.}

Keyword spotting is the always-on software component of voice-controlled devices
(smart speakers, earbuds) whose task is to detect a small
number of short spoken commands such as ``stop'' or ``next'', or a wake word.
Because it must run continuously, it is deliberately kept small and
energy-efficient; the full, resource-intensive speech recognizer is activated
only \emph{after} a keyword has been detected. Keyword spotting therefore acts as
the gateway to most voice interfaces.

\paragraph{Limitations of the closed-set approach.}
Most commercial keyword-spotting systems are trained as classifiers over a
\emph{fixed} list of words (for example, the ten commands \textit{yes, no, up,
down, \dots}).
This design has two limitations. First, the keyword set is fixed at
training time: adding a new command or word requires the manufacturer to
collect data and retrain the model. Second, a closed-set classifier is forced to
choose a label for \emph{every} input, including a cough, music, or a previously
unseen word; the device is therefore prone to \emph{false activations} when no
command was actually spoken.

\paragraph{The approach taken in this thesis.}
This thesis builds the system around \emph{metric learning}: instead of memorizing
a fixed set of labels, the model learns the more general skill of deciding whether
two audio segments contain the same word. This skill is trained once on a
large and diverse corpus of spoken words. At deployment, the user defines a
command set by providing a few recordings per word, and the system requires
no retraining. In terms of representation, each command is summarized as a single
representative point in a feature space (an \emph{embedding}); to recognize an
input, the system maps the new audio into this space and determines which command
it lies closest to. If the audio lies far from all enrolled commands, the system
concludes ``not a keyword'' --- this is the \emph{open-set} capability that lets
it ignore irrelevant speech and noise.

\paragraph{Practical value and challenges.}
This approach offers three benefits: (i) personalization without retraining,
which suits privacy-preserving on-device assistants, accessibility tools, and
customizable industrial controls; (ii) fewer false activations thanks to the
rejection capability; and (iii) a small model footprint ($\sim$0.1--0.4 million
parameters), sufficient to run offline on-device and thereby preserve privacy.
At the same time, the problem poses three main challenges. The first is
\emph{generalization}: the model must operate on words, recording devices, and
accents unseen during training, so this thesis deliberately trains on one corpus
and evaluates on a different one. The second is the \emph{open-set
boundary}: reliably rejecting the very large space of ``everything that is not a
command'' is considerably harder than a few known commands, and the
trade-off between over-rejection and under-rejection must be quantified honestly.
The third is \emph{scale}: as the training vocabulary grows from a few dozen to
nearly forty thousand words, some techniques that are effective at small scale
break down --- a phenomenon analyzed in \Cref{ch:results}.

\section{From sound to data}\label{sec:sound2data}

Sound in nature is a \emph{continuous pressure wave} that varies over time. A
computer cannot store a continuous signal, so the first step is
\emph{digitization} via two operations: \emph{sampling}, which measures the wave
amplitude at evenly spaced instants, and \emph{quantization}, which rounds each
measured value to one of a finite set of levels. The result is a discrete
sequence of numbers representing the waveform.

The sampling rate determines which frequency band the digital signal preserves.
By the \emph{Nyquist sampling theorem}, a sampling rate $f_s$ faithfully
represents only frequency components below $f_s/2$. The energy that distinguishes
phonemes in speech is concentrated below about 8\,kHz, so a rate of
$f_s = 16{,}000$ samples per second (16\,kHz) --- with a Nyquist limit of
8\,kHz --- is sufficient and has become the de-facto standard for speech tasks.
Increasing the sampling rate further mainly raises the computational cost while
adding little to word-recognition quality. Throughout this thesis we use 16\,kHz,
single-channel (mono) audio.

So that every clip has the same input size, each spoken-command segment is
standardized to exactly one second, i.e.\ a vector of $16{,}000$ numbers (the
\emph{raw waveform}): shorter clips are zero-padded at both ends and longer clips
are trimmed. Fixing the length lets us pack data into batches and keeps the
downstream front-end at a constant tensor size. \Cref{fig:audiofeat}(a) shows a
one-second waveform of the word ``yes''.

Although it represents the signal, the raw waveform is a poor input for a
classifier. First, it is \emph{very high-dimensional} ($16{,}000$ values for one
second). Second, it is \emph{not invariant to harmless transformations}: the same
word spoken slightly louder, shifted in time, or recorded on a different device
produces a very different sample sequence at the per-sample level, even though a
human still hears the same word. We therefore transform the waveform into a more
compact and stable time--frequency representation, presented in \Cref{sec:mel}.

\section{Time--frequency representations: the mel-spectrogram}\label{sec:mel}

Speech is far easier to model in the \emph{time--frequency} domain. We slide a
short analysis window (here 25--40\,ms) across the waveform in small hops
(10--20\,ms) and, for each window, compute the short-time Fourier transform
(STFT) to obtain the energy at each frequency. Two perceptual facts about human
hearing then guide the next step: we perceive pitch roughly on a logarithmic
scale, and we are more sensitive to differences at low frequencies than at high
ones. The \emph{mel scale} encodes exactly this: it warps linear frequency (Hz)
into a perceptual ``mel'' axis via
\begin{equation}
m \;=\; 2595\,\log_{10}\!\Big(1+\frac{f}{700}\Big),
\label{eq:mel}
\end{equation}
where $f$ is the frequency in Hz and $m$ the corresponding value on the mel
scale; the constants $700$ and $2595$ shape the curve so that the mel axis is
approximately linear in Hz at low frequencies and compresses logarithmically at
high frequencies, mirroring the decreasing resolution of human hearing.
Consequently, a bank of (here 40) overlapping triangular filters spaced evenly on
this mel axis pools the STFT energy into 40 \emph{mel bands}, allocating more
bands to the frequency region where human hearing is most sensitive. Taking a
logarithm of the band energies (because loudness is also perceived
logarithmically) yields the \emph{log-mel spectrogram} of
\Cref{fig:audiofeat}(b): a compact image in which the horizontal axis is time,
the vertical axis is mel frequency, and brightness is energy.

\paragraph{MFCC.}
A further classical step decorrelates the log-mel bands with a discrete cosine
transform (DCT), producing \emph{Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients} (MFCCs).
The low-order coefficients capture the slowly varying spectral envelope (which
carries most of the phonetic identity), so it is common --- and we follow this ---
to keep only the first few coefficients. \Cref{fig:audiofeat}(c) shows the
10-coefficient MFCC map we feed to one of our encoders. The whole front-end is
therefore a small, fixed transformation that turns one second of audio into a
single-channel ``image'' of shape time$\times$features, on which a convolutional
network can operate.

\paragraph{PCEN.}
The static logarithm used above is simple but not adaptive to recording
conditions. \emph{Per-Channel Energy Normalization} (PCEN)~\cite{wang2017trainable}
replaces it with a \emph{trainable}, per-band automatic-gain-control followed by a
soft compression; its parameters are learned jointly with the network. PCEN is
designed for robust, far-field detection and, as \Cref{ch:results} shows, is the
single most reliable improvement in our study. The exact equation is given in
\Cref{sec:frontends}.

\section{Keyword spotting: closed-set vs.\ open-set}\label{sec:kws-bg}

Small-footprint KWS was popularized by depthwise-separable convolutional networks
(DS-CNN)~\cite{zhang2017hello} and later refined by broadcasted-residual networks
(BC-ResNet)~\cite{kim2021bcresnet}, both of which trade accuracy against a small
parameter count for microcontroller deployment. Fundamentally these are
\emph{closed-set} classifiers: the model outputs a probability distribution over a
\emph{fixed} label set and always chooses the highest-probability label.

The core limitation is that a closed-set classifier has \emph{no ``none of the
above'' option}. The softmax function forces the probabilities over the known
classes to sum to one, so every input --- including a cough, music, or a word
outside the list --- is mapped to one of the known keywords. In laboratory
conditions, where every test sample belongs to a known class, this is not exposed.
In practice, however, the overwhelming majority of audio a device hears is
\emph{not} a keyword, so a closed-set classifier will continually mislabel
background sound, causing false activations.

The \emph{open-set} problem poses a harder and more realistic requirement: the
system must both recognize the known keywords and \emph{reject} any audio that
does not belong to the keyword set. The challenge is that the space of
``everything that is not a keyword'' is essentially infinite and cannot be
enumerated at training time, so it cannot be treated as an ordinary class.
Instead, the system needs a \emph{rejection mechanism}: it measures ``how much an
input resembles the known keywords'' and rejects it when this measure is too low.
The ability to quantify and tune the rejection threshold --- accepting correct
keywords while keeping the false-activation rate low --- is exactly what separates
a laboratory accuracy number from a deployable wake-word engine. This thesis
pursues precisely that open-set scenario, with the concrete measurement defined in
\Cref{sec:eval}.

\section{Few-shot learning and metric learning}\label{sec:fewshot-bg}

\emph{Few-shot learning} is the scenario in which a model must recognize a new
class from only a very small number of examples ($k$ shots). This is exactly the
need of user-defined KWS: the user provides a few samples per keyword and expects
the system to recognize that word immediately, without retraining. Since we cannot
train a new classifier for every vocabulary, we need a representation that allows
direct ``matching'' between a new sample and the few enrolled samples.

The natural approach is \emph{metric learning}: instead of learning a mapping from
audio to a fixed label, the network learns an \emph{embedding space} in which
distance reflects semantic similarity --- utterances of the \emph{same} word lie
close together and utterances of \emph{different} words lie far apart. Given such a
space, a new keyword is represented simply by the positions of its enrollment
samples, and recognition reduces to a nearest-point search --- independent of
whether the word appeared during training.

To make the model learn this property, training is organized into
\emph{episodes} (episodic training): each episode simulates a small few-shot
problem by sampling a group of classes and splitting each class's samples into a
\emph{support set} (playing the role of enrollment) and a \emph{query set}
(playing the role of the samples to be recognized). This mirrors the deployment
condition, so the learned skill transfers well to real use. Prototypical
networks~\cite{snell2017prototypical} represent each class by the mean
(\emph{prototype}) of its support embeddings and then classify queries by nearest
prototype; triplet embeddings~\cite{schroff2015facenet} instead optimize relative
distances directly, pulling same-class pairs together and pushing different-class
pairs apart. The Generalized End-to-End (GE2E) loss~\cite{wan2018ge2e}, originally
proposed for speaker verification, builds centroids inside each training batch
exactly as deployment builds prototypes; this match between the training objective
and the deployment procedure makes GE2E particularly well suited to few-shot
open-set KWS, which we confirm experimentally in \Cref{ch:results}.

\section{Angular-margin classification and open-set rejection}\label{sec:arc-bg}

Another family of methods for learning highly discriminative embeddings is
\emph{angular-margin classification}. The idea is to normalize both the embedding
and the weight vector of each class to unit length, so that the classification
score becomes the cosine of the angle between them; a \emph{margin} is then added
to the angle of the correct class to force the classes to separate more clearly on
the hypersphere. ArcFace~\cite{deng2019arcface} applies this principle and the
sub-center variant SCAF~\cite{deng2020subcenter} lets each class have several
sub-centers, improving robustness to noisy data; both are highly successful for
face recognition with thousands of identities. The question that arises is whether this
machinery transfers to tens of thousands of \emph{word} classes --- and this is
the question the thesis answers \emph{negatively} at extreme scale
(\Cref{ch:results}): once the number of classes reaches tens of thousands, the
angular-margin classification term dominates and collapses training unless it is
strongly down-weighted.

Besides learning a good embedding, open-set KWS needs a \emph{rejection mechanism}
to decide when an input belongs to no keyword. The simplest is a threshold on the
distance to the nearest prototype (\emph{nearest-class-mean}): accept if close
enough, reject if too far. More sophisticated methods are also studied:
OpenMax~\cite{bendale2016openmax} fits a Weibull tail distribution to the
per-class distances to estimate a ``belonging'' probability, while the energy
score~\cite{liu2020energy} provides a calibration-free measure based on a weighted
exponential sum of the distances. We adapt both to operate on prototype distances
and compare them with the plain nearest-class-mean threshold (\Cref{sec:openset}).

Finally, self-supervised speech models such as
Wav2Vec\,2.0~\cite{baevski2020wav2vec} are trained on large amounts of unlabeled
data and provide very strong representations, but they are too large to run on an
edge device. We therefore use a \emph{frozen} Wav2Vec\,2.0 only as a
\emph{teacher} in \emph{knowledge distillation}: the teacher's knowledge is
transferred to a compact \emph{student} encoder, keeping the deployed model very
small while benefiting from the teacher's representation (\Cref{sec:losses}).

\section{Problem formulation}\label{sec:formulation}
\reviewnote{Short formalization so the equations referenced later are defined.}

Let $f_\theta:\R^{T\times F}\!\to\!\R^{d}$ be an encoder mapping a one-second
representation to a unit-norm embedding. The prototype of a keyword $w$ is the
mean of its $k$ support embeddings,
\begin{equation}
p_w \;=\; \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} f_\theta\!\big(x^{(w)}_i\big).
\label{eq:proto}
\end{equation}
In \Cref{eq:proto}, $x^{(w)}_i$ is the $i$-th enrollment sample of keyword $w$,
$f_\theta(\cdot)$ its embedding, and $k$ the number of enrollment shots (here
$k{=}10$). Intuitively, the prototype $p_w$ is the central representative point of
a keyword in embedding space; averaging several shots cancels the random variation
of individual utterances and yields a stable description of the word --- this is
precisely the user's ``enrollment'' operation.

A query $x$ is assigned to the nearest prototype, with open-set score equal to the
negated minimum distance,
\begin{equation}
\hat w(x)=\arg\min_{w} d\!\big(f_\theta(x),p_w\big),\qquad
s(x)=-\!\min_{w} d\!\big(f_\theta(x),p_w\big),
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
and accepted as $\hat w(x)$ iff $s(x)\ge\tau$, otherwise labelled
\texttt{unknown}. In \Cref{eq:score}, $d(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the distance in embedding
space and $\tau$ the rejection threshold. The left expression determines
\emph{which} keyword is nearest; the right expression turns the distance to the
nearest prototype into a \emph{confidence score} $s(x)$ (close means high, far
means low). The open-set decision is therefore intuitive: if the confidence
exceeds $\tau$, accept the nearest keyword; otherwise treat the input as
``unknown''. Raising or lowering $\tau$ is exactly the knob that trades off missed
keywords against false activations.

\paragraph{Worked example: enrollment, matching, and rejection.}
Suppose a user wants the device to recognize the command ``stop''.
\emph{Enrollment}: the user records $k{=}10$ clips of ``stop''; each clip is
passed through the encoder into an embedding, and their mean gives the prototype
$p_{\text{stop}}$ via \Cref{eq:proto}. Suppose the system has only this one
keyword and the threshold is set so that the maximum accepted distance is $0.9$
(i.e.\ $s(x)\ge\tau \Leftrightarrow d\le 0.9$). \emph{Matching}: when the user
says ``stop'', the query embedding lands close to $p_{\text{stop}}$, say
$d=0.45$; since $0.45\le 0.9$, the query is \emph{accepted} and labelled ``stop''.
\emph{Rejection}: when the microphone picks up an unfamiliar sound --- for
instance a piece of music or the out-of-vocabulary word ``elephant'' --- its
embedding lies far from every prototype, say $d=1.6$; since $1.6>0.9$, the system
\emph{rejects} it and returns \texttt{unknown} instead of forcing a wrong ``stop''
label. This distance--threshold mechanism, applied simultaneously to many
keywords, is the foundation of the open-set recognition in this thesis.

\section{Datasets and Training Protocol}\label{sec:data}

\textbf{Training corpus (MSWC English):} We train on the English subset of the
Multilingual Spoken Words Corpus, which after filtering for $\ge$ minimum clips
contains 37,387 training words (and 763 validation words), drawn from a metadata
pool of $\sim$38,150 words and $\sim$6.6\,M clips. To study scale we form caps of
$\{20, 50, 220, 620\}$ clips per word --- roughly $\{0.53, 0.94, 2.05, 2.99\}$\,M
training clips --- and two reduced regimes: a Top-500 legacy split (450 train / 50
val words, evaluation on words ranked 501+ with $\ge 1000$ utterances) and an
MLCommons micro-set (31 keywords) used only for pipeline validation and
architecture screening. \NEW{The three vocabulary regimes span more than three
orders of magnitude (\Cref{fig:vocabscale}); this range is what makes the
scale effects in \Cref{ch:results} observable.}

\NEW{The three regimes in \Cref{fig:vocabscale} serve three purposes. The
\emph{micro-set} (31 words) is small enough to train in minutes and is used to
verify that the pipeline is correct and to screen architecture choices quickly
before committing to expensive runs. \emph{Top-500} is an intermediate scale
under the legacy split, which lets us compare against prior work that uses the
same setup. \emph{Full MSWC} (37,387 words) is the headline regime and reflects
the realistic condition of a very large vocabulary; some phenomena --- such as
the SCAF collapse in \Cref{ch:results} --- appear only at this scale.}

\textbf{Episodic sampling and augmentation:} Episodes draw 30 classes $\times$ 20
utterances (only classes with $\ge 20$ clips are eligible). Online augmentation
mixes DEMAND background noise (probability 0.5, SNR sampled in $[0, 10]$\,dB) and
applies SpecAugment (one frequency mask of width 6, one time mask of width 8);
gain perturbation ($\pm6$\,dB) and time-shift ($\le 100$\,ms) are applied with
probability 0.5. \NEW{\Cref{fig:augment} illustrates these augmentations on a real
recording: additive background noise at 5\,dB SNR fills the quiet regions, and the
SpecAugment frequency/time masks (black bands) force the encoder not to rely on any
single band or instant.}

\textbf{Evaluation corpus (GSC v2):} We evaluate cross-corpus on Google Speech
Commands v2. Enrollment (support) samples are drawn from the official
\code{validation\_list.txt}. Query samples are drawn according to the evaluation
split: in the ``test'' split they come from \code{testing\_list.txt}, whereas in
the ``dev'' split they come from the files \emph{not} listed in either
\code{validation\_list.txt} or \code{testing\_list.txt}. Because the three lists
are disjoint, there is no leakage between enrollment and query samples. A synthetic
\code{\_silence\_} class is generated from random one-second crops of
\code{\_background\_noise\_}, with disjoint crops for support and query.

\textbf{EdgeSpot-exact protocol:} The headline protocol partitions GSC's 35 words
into 11 positive classes --- the ten command words \{yes, no, up, down, left,
right, on, off, stop, go\} plus \code{\_silence\_} --- and 25 negative (unknown)
spoken words. Each run enrolls $k{=}10$ shots per positive word, computes
prototypes, and scores up to 50 query samples per word (a fixed-seed subsample).
We report the mean$\pm$std over 100 randomized runs; randomness comes from
enrollment-shot selection (the known/unknown vocabulary itself is fixed across
runs). ``test100'' denotes 100 runs on the test split, ``dev30'' denotes 30 runs
on the dev split.

\section{System Architecture}\label{sec:arch}

This section describes the three components that make up the encoder and the
decision procedure: \Cref{sec:frontends} presents the feature front-ends that turn
the waveform into a time--frequency representation; \Cref{sec:encoders} describes

signal from input, through the network, to the embedding consumed by the

\subsection{Feature front-ends}\label{sec:frontends}

(16,000 samples). We support two spectral parameterizations, summarized in
\Cref{tab:features}.

\textbf{MFCC (DSCNN front-end):} We compute 40 Mel-frequency cepstral
coefficients with a 1024-point FFT, 640-sample (40\,ms) Hann window, 320-sample
(20\,ms) hop, and \code{center=False}; the Mel filterbank uses Slaney scale and
Slaney area normalization, and the log is taken as $10\log_{10}(\cdot)$ followed
by an orthonormal DCT-II. We retain only the first 10 coefficients and transpose
to a tensor of shape $(1,47,10)=(\text{channel}, T_{\text{frames}},
\text{features})$, where $T = 47 = \lfloor(16000-1024)/320\rfloor + 1$.

\textbf{Log-mel (EdgeSpot/BC-ResNet front-end):} We use 40 Mel bands with a
512-point FFT, 400-sample (25\,ms) window, 160-sample (10\,ms) hop and
\code{center=True}, producing $(1,40,101)$. The log compression is delegated to
the PCEN layer.

\textbf{Trainable PCEN:} Per-channel energy normalization~\cite{wang2017trainable}
applies a causal first-order IIR smoother $M_t = (1-s)M_{t-1} + sE_t$ followed by
adaptive gain and dynamic-range compression,
\begin{equation}
\text{PCEN}(E_t)=\Big(\frac{E_t}{(\epsilon+M_t)^{\alpha}}+\delta\Big)^{r}-\delta^{r},
\label{eq:pcen}
\end{equation}
with learnable $\alpha,\delta,r,s$ (initialized to $0.96, 2.0, 0.5, 0.04$;
$\epsilon{=}10^{-6}$). In \Cref{eq:pcen}, $E_t$ is the mel-band energy at time
frame $t$ and $M_t$ its time-smoothed version (an estimate of the local background
level). The roles of the components are as follows: the division
$E_t/(\epsilon+M_t)^{\alpha}$ performs \emph{automatic gain control} ---
normalizing each band by its recent background so that abrupt changes (consonant
onsets) are emphasized and steady components (background noise) are suppressed; the
exponent $r$ together with the offset $\delta$ performs a soft \emph{dynamic-range
compression} in place of the static logarithm. Because $\alpha,\delta,r,s$ are
learned jointly with the network, PCEN adapts to the recording conditions instead
of using a fixed compression function --- which is why it is more robust and, as
\Cref{ch:results} shows, the single most reliable improvement in this study.

\textbf{DSCNN-L:} A depthwise-separable CNN built from a size table: an initial
$\text{Conv}$ $(1{\to}276,$ $10{\times}4,$ stride $2{\times}1)$ with
TensorFlow-style asymmetric ``same'' padding, followed by five depthwise separable
blocks (276 channels, $3{\times}3$ kernels; the first block strides $2{\times}2$).
All but the last block use BatchNorm+ReLU; the last block uses an (affine-free)
LayerNorm. A global average pool yields the embedding directly, so the embedding
dimension equals the channel width, $d = 276$ (there is no final linear
projection). DSCNN-L has $\approx 0.413$\,M parameters.

\textbf{EdgeSpot-Full ($\tau{=}4$):} A width-scaled edge encoder operating on the
$(1,40,101)$ mel/PCEN input. A $5{\times}5$ stride-$(2,1)$ stem feeds four stages
of fused-temporal and broadcasted-residual ``lite'' blocks with increasing
temporal dilation $(1,2,4,8)$; a depthwise frequency-collapse block averages over
frequency to a $(C,T)$ sequence; a depthwise positional convolution and a
single-head temporal self-attention block ($d = 64$) model long-range temporal
structure; finally a depthwise temporal head and global pooling produce a
64-dimensional embedding. EdgeSpot-Full $\tau{=}4$ has $\approx 0.131$\,M
parameters, $\sim$3.2$\times$ smaller than DSCNN-L. A lighter EdgeSpot-Lite
variant and a clean BC-ResNet-FS baseline (no attention) are also provided.

\subsection{Metric-learning objectives}\label{sec:losses}

All objectives are trained episodically: each episode samples $n_{\text{cls}}{=}30$
classes with $n_{\text{spp}}{=}20$ utterances each (600 embeddings/episode).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{fig_model_tradeoff.png}
\caption{\NEW{Accuracy vs.\ model size. DSCNN-L leads on accuracy; EdgeSpot-Full
reaches a competitive point at one third the parameters; KD lifts the compact
model further at low data. (Accuracy numbers are detailed in \Cref{ch:results}.)}
\emph{Generated from project results.}}
\label{fig:tradeoff}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Triplet:} Semi-hard triplet loss~\cite{schroff2015facenet} with Euclidean
distance, $\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{N}\sum\max(0, d(a,p)-d(a,n)+m)$, with
hardest-positive mining and FaceNet semi-hard negative mining. Here $a$ is the
anchor, $p$ a sample of the \emph{same} word (positive), $n$ a sample of a
\emph{different} word (negative), and $m$ the margin. The loss forces the
anchor--positive distance to be smaller than the anchor--negative distance by at
least $m$; the $\max(0,\cdot)$ means a triplet is penalized only when this
condition is violated, i.e.\ the model learns only from ``hard'' triplets.

\textbf{Sub-center ArcFace (SCAF):} An angular-margin softmax with $K{=}3$
sub-centers per class. With normalized weights $W\in\R^{(C\cdot K)\times d}$, the
per-class logit is the max cosine over its sub-centers,
$\cos\theta_c = \max_{j\le K} w_{c,j}^{\top} z$; the additive margin $m{=}0.5$ is
applied to the target class and logits are scaled by $s{=}30$ before
cross-entropy. Here $z$ is the normalized embedding and $w_{c,j}$ the $j$-th
sub-center of class $c$; taking the max over sub-centers lets one word have several
distinct ``pronunciation styles''. The margin $m$ forces the correct class to be
clearly superior in angle, while the scale $s$ amplifies the logits before
softmax --- and it is exactly these two factors that, multiplied across tens of
thousands of classes, cause the collapse analyzed in \Cref{ch:results}.

\textbf{GE2E (centroid):} Generalized end-to-end loss~\cite{wan2018ge2e} that
mirrors deployment: within an episode each class is split into support/query; the
centroid is the $\ell_2$-normalized support mean and queries are classified by
scaled cosine similarity to all centroids with learnable scale and bias, optimized
by cross-entropy. GE2E directly optimizes the enroll-then-match objective of
\Cref{eq:proto,eq:score}.

\textbf{Cosine knowledge distillation (KD):} We additionally distill from a frozen
Wav2Vec\,2.0-base teacher (hidden layer 16, mean-pooled, projected to $d{=}64$ by
a sub-center-ArcFace-trained head). The distillation loss is a pure
embedding-cosine term,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}} = 1 - \frac{1}{N}\sum_i
\cos\!\big(f_\theta(x_i),\, g_{\text{teacher}}(x_i)\big),
\label{eq:kd}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ no temperature/soft-label term. In \Cref{eq:kd}, $f_\theta(x_i)$ is the
student embedding and $g_{\text{teacher}}(x_i)$ the frozen Wav2Vec\,2.0 teacher
embedding for the same input $x_i$. Since $\cos$ equals 1 when two vectors point in
the same direction, minimizing $\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}$ is equivalent to aligning
the student embedding with the teacher embedding --- i.e.\ forcing the small model
to imitate the representation space of the large one. Teacher embeddings are
precomputed offline and looked up by file path during training, so the additional
training cost is negligible.

\textbf{Hybrid combinations:} We form weighted sums, e.g.\ \code{scaf\_ge2e},
\code{kd\_ge2e}, \code{kd\_scaf}, \code{kd\_scaf\_ge2e}. A crucial implementation
detail (and a key empirical finding, \Cref{ch:results}) is the SCAF weight: under
KD it is set to $5\times10^{-5}$ while GE2E/KD weights are 1.0. The very small
SCAF weight prevents the high-magnitude angular-margin classification loss from
dominating the centroid/cosine terms.

\NEW{\textbf{Optimization.} Unless noted otherwise, every model is trained with the
Adam optimizer (initial learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$). The
gradient norm is clipped at $5.0$ (gradient clipping) to avoid overly large update
steps that would destabilize training, especially for the hybrid losses. The
learning rate decays on a \emph{cosine-annealing schedule with warm restarts}
(CosineAnnealingWarmRestarts): it follows a cosine curve downward and is then reset
periodically, with a first cycle of $T_0{=}10$ epochs, each subsequent cycle
$T_{\text{mult}}{=}2\times$ longer than the previous one, and a floor of
$\eta_{\min}{=}10^{-5}$. For reproducibility we fix all random seeds ($=42$) and
enable cuDNN deterministic mode. Per-experiment epoch/episode budgets are stated in
\Cref{ch:results}.}

\section{Open-set rejection heads}\label{sec:openset}
\reviewnote{Subsection documenting the four rejection heads.}

All heads share the prototype interface and differ only in the rejection score;
the predicted keyword is always the argmin-distance class (\Cref{eq:score}).
(i) \textbf{Nearest-class-mean (NCM, ``Direct $\ell_2$'')} --- our default ---
rejects when the minimum prototype distance exceeds a threshold, using raw
distances with no probability normalization.
(ii) \textbf{OpenMax}~\cite{bendale2016openmax} fits a Weibull tail to
support-to-prototype distances and uses $1-\mathrm{CDF}$ as the membership score.
(iii) \textbf{Energy}~\cite{liu2020energy} treats $-d_i$ as logits and uses the
free energy $E(x)=T\log\sum_i\exp(-d_i/T)$ as the acceptance score.
(iv) \textbf{Calibrated} thresholds ($\mu+2\sigma$ of support distances) drive
per-word confusion analysis.

\section{Evaluation methodology}\label{sec:eval}

This section defines the evaluation metrics \emph{before} they are used in
\Cref{ch:results}. We treat the open-set problem as a binary
keyword-vs-non-keyword detection augmented by a multiclass identity track, and
build all metrics on the ROC curve. Two base concepts first: \far{} (False-Accept
Rate) is the fraction of \emph{non-keyword} audio that is wrongly accepted, and
\frr{} (False-Reject Rate) is the fraction of \emph{genuine} keywords that are
wrongly rejected. The symbol $\uparrow$ means \emph{higher is better} and
$\downarrow$ means \emph{lower is better}.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{AUC} ($\uparrow$): area under the ROC of the open-set scores
(\Cref{eq:score}); threshold-free, it measures the general separability of
keywords from non-keywords.
\item \textbf{EER} ($\downarrow$): equal-error rate, the operating point
minimizing $|\far-\frr|$, reported as $(\far+\frr)/2$.
\item \textbf{FRR@FAR} ($\downarrow$): false-reject rate at the largest threshold
whose false-accept rate does not exceed the target (1\% or 5\%).
\item \textbf{Open-set ACC@FAR} ($\uparrow$): the headline accuracy. A known query
is correct iff it is accepted ($s\ge\tau_{\far}$) and the argmin label is correct;
an unknown query is correct iff it is rejected. Reported at $\far\in\{1\%, 5\%\}$.
\item \textbf{Keyword ACC} ($\uparrow$): closed-set nearest-prototype accuracy over
known queries only (ignores rejection).
\item \textbf{F1} ($\uparrow$): binary keyword-vs-unknown F1 at the EER threshold.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Interpretation caveat.} AUC and EER are threshold-free, whereas ACC@FAR
and FRR@FAR use a threshold derived from the evaluation ROC (an oracle operating
point), not transferred from a held-out calibration split. We therefore treat
ACC@FAR as a comparison metric across systems under matched conditions, and report
AUC/EER/F1 jointly so that degenerate operating points are visible. A system that
rejects nearly everything can score a high open-set ACC against many negatives
while missing all positives; such cases are exposed by $\frr\to100\%$ and
$\text{F1}\to0$.

\section{Experimental design: what we measure}\label{sec:expdesign}
\reviewnote{New section. It states the four questions the experiments answer
(without giving results --- results are Chapter~4).}

The experiments are organized around four questions:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=2pt]
\item[\textbf{Q1 --- Which design axis matters?}] We train all
$2{\times}2{\times}4 = 16$ combinations of \{DSCNN-L, EdgeSpot-Full\} $\times$
\{MFCC, PCEN\} $\times$ \{Triplet, SCAF, GE2E, SCAF$+$GE2E\} on full-vocabulary
MSWC and isolate each axis with \emph{paired deltas} (changing one factor while
holding the others fixed).
\item[\textbf{Q2 --- How does performance scale?}] Fixing the best front-end and
loss, we sweep the per-word cap $\{20,50,220,620\}$ and grow the vocabulary from
31 to 37,387 words, watching for both saturation and failure.
\item[\textbf{Q3 --- Can distillation replace data?}] We compare GE2E against the
cosine-KD objective at matched data caps.
\item[\textbf{Q4 --- What is the best deployable system?}] We consolidate the
strongest configurations per data regime and identify the best overall and best
compact operating points.
\end{description}

\section{Deployment: streaming and demonstrator}\label{sec:deploy}
\reviewnote{New section documenting the on-device/streaming pipeline and the web
demonstrator.}

Beyond the core encoder, the system includes a CPU streaming pipeline and an
offline web demonstrator (\Cref{fig:demo}). Streaming performs sliding-window
detection (1\,s window, 0.5\,s stride) gated by Silero Voice Activity Detection
(VAD): only windows that VAD marks as speech are embedded and matched, reducing
computation and false activations on silence. A backend-agnostic decision
\emph{state machine} (\Cref{alg:sm}) stabilizes raw candidates into clean events
using majority-vote smoothing over a short window, a top-2 margin check, and a
cooldown to suppress duplicate firings. Optional modules --- a
spectral-gating/SpeechBrain denoiser and an ECAPA-TDNN speaker-verification gate
--- follow the same fail-open, runtime-toggle pattern. The pipeline is designed to
a per-component CPU budget (VAD $<5$\,ms/chunk, MFCC $<5$\,ms, encoder $<10$\,ms,
classifier $<1$\,ms; core $<21$\,ms; $<71$\,ms with all enhancements); a measured
on-device benchmark is left to future work.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Streaming decision state machine (smoothing $+$ cooldown).}
\label{alg:sm}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require window $W$, votes $V$, margin $m_{\min}$, cooldown $C$
\State buffer $\gets$ deque(maxlen $=W$);\ $t_{\text{last}}\gets-\infty$
\For{each candidate $c$ with span $[t_s,t_e]$}
  \If{$t_s - t_{\text{last}} < C$} \State emit \textsc{cooldown};\ \textbf{continue} \EndIf
  \If{$\lnot c.\text{detected}$ \textbf{or} $c.\text{kw}{=}$\texttt{unknown}
       \textbf{or} $c.\text{margin}<m_{\min}$}
     \State clear buffer; emit \textsc{rejected};\ \textbf{continue}
  \EndIf
  \State push $c$;\ $k^\star\gets$ majority keyword in buffer
  \If{votes$(k^\star)\ge V$}
     \State $a\gets\arg\max_{\text{conf}}\{c\in\text{buffer}: c.\text{kw}{=}k^\star\}$
     \State $t_{\text{last}}\gets a.t_e$; clear buffer; emit \textsc{detected}$(k^\star)$
  \Else\ \State emit \textsc{scoring} \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{The SCAF collapse at 37k classes.}
The most striking result of the scale study is a qualitative failure. On the
31-class micro-set and the $\sim$500-class Top-500 split, SCAF and SCAF$+$GE2E are
excellent (AUC $\approx 95$; \Cref{sec:res-q4}). However, on the full
37,387-class vocabulary with the default SCAF weight of 1.0, training collapses
from epoch~2: validation AUC pins at 0.5000 and the in-episode GE2E accuracy falls
to $\approx 0.033$ ($\approx 1/30$, i.e.\ chance). The cross-corpus GSC metrics are
correspondingly degenerate (\Cref{fig:scafcollapse}): AUC 50, F1 0,
$\frr{=}100\%$, keyword ACC 9.09\% ($={}$$1/11$ classes), while the open-set
ACC@1\%FAR stays at a \emph{misleading} 69.4\% --- a reject-all artefact that AUC,
F1 and FRR immediately expose. The mechanism is a gradient-magnitude imbalance: the sub-center ArcFace
logit scale ($s{=}30$) multiplied across $\sim$$3.7\times10^4$ classes produces a
classification gradient that dwarfs the centroid/cosine terms, driving all
embeddings to a trivial solution. The same hybrid does \emph{not} collapse under
KD, where the SCAF weight is $5\times10^{-5}$. The practical remedies we verify
are: (i) reduce the SCAF weight to $\sim$$5\times10^{-5}$, (ii) warm up the
margin/scale, or (iii) drop the classification term and use GE2E or KD. This is a
cautionary result: angular-margin classification losses that are state-of-the-art
at $10^2$--$10^3$ classes do not transfer naively to $10^4$-class word embedding.

\paragraph{Practical implication.} Both Q2 findings have clear operational
consequences. Saturation near $\sim$2\,M clips means that, beyond a certain point,
collecting more clips per word barely improves accuracy --- so a data-collection
budget should prioritize \emph{diversity of words and recording conditions} over
more clips of the same word. The SCAF collapse at large scale is a cautionary
tale: a loss that is excellent at small scale can destroy training at real scale
unless re-tuned, so design choices must be re-validated whenever the scale
increases rather than assumed to still hold.

\chapter{Conclusion}\label{ch:conclusion}
\reviewnote{This chapter ($\sim$2 pages): current status, what is good, what is
weak, and future work.}

This chapter closes the thesis by reviewing the whole arc: from the problem posed
in \Cref{ch:intro}, through the method in \Cref{ch:method}, to the experimental
evidence in \Cref{ch:results}. We summarize the current status of the system,
state its strengths and weaknesses honestly, and outline the next directions that
follow directly from the limitations observed.

\section{Current status}\label{sec:status}

This thesis set out to build a keyword-spotting system that escapes the two
structural limits of classical wake-word engines --- a frozen vocabulary and the
inability to abstain --- and to understand, rather than merely report, what makes
such a system work as the training vocabulary grows. We delivered a complete,
reproducible, dependency-light pipeline: two compact encoders (DSCNN-L at
$\approx$0.41\,M and EdgeSpot-Full at $\approx$0.13\,M parameters), three feature
front-ends, four metric-learning objectives, four open-set rejection heads, a
CPU streaming engine, and an offline web demonstrator. Every system is trained on
MSWC English and evaluated cross-corpus on GSC v2 under a single strict
EdgeSpot-exact 10-shot protocol with 100 randomized runs, so the numbers compare
fairly.

The headline outcome is that few-shot open-set KWS is viable and even strong at
true vocabulary scale: training once on $37{,}387$ words, the best configuration
(DSCNN-L $+$ PCEN $+$ GE2E) reaches $86.36\%$ open-set accuracy at a strict
$1\%$ false-accept rate and $95.21$ AUC on a corpus it was never trained on, while
a three-times-smaller edge model stays within a few points. A user can define an
arbitrary keyword set from ten examples per word, with no retraining, and the
system can decline to answer on unknown speech and silence.

\section{What is good, what is weak}\label{sec:goodbad}

\paragraph{What works.} Three findings are robust and transferable. (1) The
trainable PCEN front-end is the most reliable single improvement, and it helps the
smaller encoder most --- a useful lever precisely where the parameter budget is
tightest. (2) The centroid GE2E objective, which optimizes the same
enroll-then-match computation used at deployment, consistently beats both triplet
and classification objectives for open-set generalization. (3) A frozen
Wav2Vec\,2.0 teacher distilled with a simple cosine loss lets the compact model
reach GE2E-level accuracy with roughly half the data, which matters when data or
compute are limited.

\paragraph{What is weak / what we learned the hard way.} The clearest negative
result is the collapse of the sub-center ArcFace (SCAF) classification objective at
$10^4$-class scale: a loss that is excellent on tens to hundreds of classes drives
the embedding to a trivial solution on tens of thousands of classes unless its
weight is cut by four orders of magnitude. This is an important cautionary lesson
for anyone scaling angular-margin losses to large label spaces. Two additional
limitations bound the present claims. First, the ACC@FAR/FRR@FAR operating points
are read from the evaluation ROC (an oracle threshold) rather than transferred
from an independent calibration split; we mitigate this by always co-reporting
threshold-free AUC/EER/F1, but a calibrated, deployment-time threshold study is
still owed. Second, enrollment is fixed at $k{=}10$ shots and the streaming
latency figures are design budgets, not measured field benchmarks; there is also
no quantitative denoising ablation.

\section{Future work}\label{sec:future}

Four directions follow directly from the limitations above.
(1) \emph{$k$-shot sensitivity:} sweep $k\in\{1,3,5,10\}$ to chart the
accuracy-vs-enrollment-effort curve, which is the metric end users actually feel.
(2) \emph{Calibrated thresholds:} learn or transfer the open-set threshold from a
held-out split and report deployment-realistic ACC@FAR, closing the oracle gap.
(3) \emph{On-device benchmark:} measure end-to-end latency, false-alarms-per-hour
and miss-rate on target hardware, plus an SNR-sweep denoising ablation, to validate
the design budgets.
(4) \emph{Stabilizing classification at scale:} margin/scale warm-up, curriculum,
or sub-center pruning to recover the discriminative power of angular-margin losses
without the collapse --- potentially combining SCAF's tight clusters with GE2E's
deployment-matched objective at full vocabulary.

In summary, the thesis demonstrates a deployable, retraining-free, open-set
keyword spotter and, just as importantly, a clear empirical map of which design
choices help, where they saturate, and where they fail as the problem scales.
